12:T984,Kate Adamala's lab at University of Minnesota created synthetic cells called "spudcells" (Adamala, Polish, joked they should name them after potatoes) that underwent a complete cell cycle for the first time: growth, DNA replication, and division. The cells are constructed by packing nonliving biological components—a genome, enzymes for DNA replication and protein synthesis, lipid membranes, and supply packages of nutrients—inside liposomes. The breakthrough was solving cell division, which had stuck the field. Rather than recreate the cytoskeleton's complex reorganization, Adamala adapted a mechanism from Reinhard Lipowsky's work: engineered membrane proteins that attract other proteins to crowd and physically bend the membrane until the cell splits. The team combined DNA replication systems (from Mutschler and Danelon), commercial enzyme packs, and feeder liposomes delivering sugar, ribosomes, and tRNA. Cells grew larger and divided faster when given genetic variants, showing early signs of selection, though true evolution wasn't demonstrated since the team created variation synthetically rather than through mutations. Experts like John Glass (J. Craig Venter Institute) called it "a watershed event," and Job Boekhoven noted the division mechanism was "a huge achievement." Jack Szostak cautioned the system isn't self-sustaining—cells can't make their own ribosomes and require constant feeding. The paper was posted to bioRxiv on July 2 but is not yet peer-reviewed.
