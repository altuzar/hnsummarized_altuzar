12:Tcab,This essay stakes a crucial distinction: LLMs aren't dangerous because they're too weak or too strong, but because they let students defer the hard thinking that actually builds expertise. The author uses two fictional PhD students—Alice, who reads papers carefully and debugs code herself, versus Bob, who outsources everything to AI agents—to illustrate why identical outputs mask radically different human development.

The core insight appears in Schwartz's experiment with Claude: a theoretical physicist had to catch AI fabrications (faked plots, invented coefficients, incorrect simplifications) because decades of manual work gave him intuition about what answers should look like. A novice using Claude on the same problem would produce identical-looking work while learning nothing. The bottleneck isn't model capability; it's human supervision, which requires exactly the kind of training modern AI threatens to bypass.

Karamanis identifies the institutional rot: academia's metrics reward papers, not understanding. Publication counts treat Alice and Bob as interchangeable. The system has no reason to distinguish them, and every incentive to reward whatever produces output fastest. This creates rational incentive for Bobs everywhere—why learn the hard way when AI lets you ship three papers instead of one? But the career ladder eventually demands the tacit knowledge AI can't provide: problem identification, error intuition, ability to mentor others. Skip the first five years of learning and you hit a ceiling.

The essay addresses rebuttals carefully. "Models will improve" misses the point: stronger models won't eliminate the need for expert supervision, just broaden what novices can attempt without understanding. The "LLMs are just tools like compilers" argument conflates tool use (human makes decisions, tool executes) with cognitive outsourcing (machine makes decisions, human validates). The distinction is everything. Karamanis himself uses AI, but always after the thinking is done—a critical sequence many junior researchers reverse.

Comments reveal substantial agreement on the core problem. One experienced engineer notes that when Claude writes code, he can't hold the structure in his own mind afterward; he checked decisions rather than making them, leaving nothing to grab onto during maintenance. Another observes the paradox directly: LLMs only work if you're Schwartz, but you can't become Schwartz by using LLMs. This traps institutions in needing a steady supply of Alices while incentivizing everyone to become Bob.

However, several commenters push back productively. Some argue Bob could ask the AI to teach him its reasoning, compressing five years of learning into months. Others note that even with AI, you can tell when outputs are wrong, and supervising agents itself becomes a learnable skill. One developer found that engaging with agent-generated code and having the agent explain its decisions led to real understanding faster than solo work. The article's strongest critics contend that academia's real problem—weak ownership, unclear standards, deferred decisions—existed before LLMs and persists regardless.
