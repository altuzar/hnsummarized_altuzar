The system generates deterministic, seeded medieval islands with roads, rivers, forests, villages, and cliffs across 4,100 hex tiles arranged in 19 hexagonal grids, completing generation in ~20 seconds.

The core mechanism mirrors the board game Carcassonne: tiles have edges with types (road, grass, water, cliff), and adjacent tiles must have matching edges. With hex geometry's 6 edges and multiple states (30 tile types × 6 rotations × 5 elevation levels = 900 possible states per cell), the combinatorial explosion is significant. The solver picks the most-constrained cell (lowest entropy), randomly selects a valid tile, then propagates constraints outward, removing incompatible neighbor states. The tricky part is backtracking: when the solver boxes itself into an unsolvable corner, it undoes decisions (tracking deltas allows cheap rewinding). The author limits backtracking to 500 attempts per grid.

Because large monolithic grids fail frequently due to accumulated constraint conflicts, the map splits into 19 modular grids with shared borders acting as fixed constraints. When neighboring grids create incompatibilities, a three-layer recovery system engages: (1) Unfixing converts conflicting fixed border tiles back into solvable cells; (2) Local-WFC re-solves a small 19-cell radius-2 region to find compatible boundaries (the breakthrough insight—change the problem instead of solving the impossible); (3) Drop-and-hide places mountains to cover seams (cliffs match any neighbor). Elevation adds a third dimension, requiring slope tiles to bridge level changes so roads don't dead-end into cliffs.

The article details rendering optimizations: BatchedMesh combines thousands of tiles into single draw calls, one shared material with a palette texture atlas eliminates shader switches, dynamic shadow frustum fitting prevents wasted resolution. Post-processing (GTAO ambient occlusion, depth-of-field tilt-shift, vignette) creates a miniature diorama aesthetic. Water caustics use scrolling textures; coastal waves radiate from shorelines via a dilated/blurred coast mask, with a CPU-side "surroundedness" probe fixing the cove problem where wave bands stretched awkwardly in concave inlets. Perlin noise (separate from WFC) handles tree and building clustering—WFC fails at large-scale patterns, producing scattered rather than clustered features. The author used 3D assets from KayKit's Medieval Hexagon pack, adding custom Blender-made connectors with precise 2-unit-wide tile constraints.

Hex coordinates use cube coordinates (q, r, s where s = -q-r) for trivial neighbor finding, avoiding the complexity of offset coordinates.
