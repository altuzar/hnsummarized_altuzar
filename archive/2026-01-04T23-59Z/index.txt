The linked page content was unavailable, but the HN comment thread thoroughly covers the explanation. The core insight, articulated by multiple commenters, is that ordinary least squares (OLS) regression makes a specific assumption: only the Y variable contains noise, while X values are treated as exact and error-free. Under this assumption, OLS minimizes the sum of squared vertical distances from points to the line, which is mathematically optimal for making unbiased predictions of Y given X.

However, when people visually inspect the scatter plot, their intuition applies a different mental model: both X and Y have measurement error. Under that assumption, the "best fit" should minimize distances perpendicular to the line (or the shortest distance to the line), which corresponds to techniques like Principal Component Analysis or Total Least Squares. These methods treat the fitting problem geometrically rather than through the lens of predicting one variable from another.

Key points from comments: The asymmetry is not a bug—it reflects that regression is fundamentally directional (predicting Y from X) rather than geometric. If you regress Y on X, you get one line; if you regress X on Y, you get a different line (steeper). Deming regression or Total Least Squares are presented as alternatives when measurement error in both variables is realistic. One commenter noted that in real-world data collection, scenarios where X has no error are hard to identify, suggesting OLS's assumption is often violated in practice. Another mentions this phenomenon as "regression dilution."

The thread also covers why least squares specifically: it's mathematically convenient and has nice theoretical properties (unbiasedness of predictions), though commenters note other loss functions exist and produce different results.
