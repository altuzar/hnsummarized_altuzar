Ronacher frames the deeper tension as a shift from "software as a deterministic machine to software as an organism." Traditional software engineering valued understanding: you could peel back layers, understand invariants, know which changes were safe. Now we're increasingly treating systems like doctors treat patients—observing symptoms, forming hypotheses, trying remedies, looping with machines on diagnosis and remediation. Some engineers already merge code they can't explain and rely on machines to triage security reports, propose root causes, and land patches without human review. This works for some software, but it means accepting that we no longer comprehend the whole system.

The uncomfortable part: opting out may be impossible. Security researchers and attackers will loop continuously against your software with automated probes. Competitors who orchestrate machines effectively will out-build you through raw speed. Some startups will achieve the output of 50-person teams with five people. If users are happy with the result, does maintainability matter? Not all domains are equally at risk—some punish sloppiness and demand trust. But many reward speed and coverage.

Codebases built by loops, reviewed by loops, patched by loops will assume machine participation in their maintenance model.
