12:Tc28,Michael Geist analyzes Canada's Bill C-22, a reintroduction of lawful access legislation after the government abandoned similar provisions in Bill C-2. The bill has two parts: one governing law enforcement data access and another (SAAIA) establishing surveillance infrastructure requirements.

On data access, C-22 represents significant improvement. It scraps C-2's dangerously broad warrantless disclosure power covering all service providers (physicians, lawyers, ISPs). The new "confirmation of service" demand lets police ask telecom providers only whether they serve a specific person, with more detailed subscriber information requiring judicial production orders at the "reasonable grounds to suspect" threshold. This is narrower than C-2 and addresses police complaints about wasted investigative work.

However, the bill's SAAIA section is deeply problematic and largely unchanged from C-2, with expansions. It requires all "electronic service providers" (ISPs, Internet platforms like Google and Meta, WhatsApp) to assist law enforcement with surveillance capability testing and direct network access. Critically, providers must retain metadata—transmission data, timestamps—for up to a year for "core providers," and all requests must be kept secret. A new warrant exemption allows judges to withhold warrant copies if "justified in the circumstances," creating a subjective loophole. The broad ESP definition captures essentially any online service providing electronic communications.

Geist notes genuine risks: the systemic vulnerability exemption appears insufficient, potentially compromising network security; the secrecy makes public accountability impossible; international data-sharing frameworks like the Budapest Convention's Second Protocol and the CLOUD Act integrate Canadian infrastructure into global surveillance; and data breaches on retained metadata could be catastrophic. The bill also imposes obligations on private contractors without addressing liability.
