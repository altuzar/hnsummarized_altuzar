Basic techniques: Distance culling (simple max distance, risks pop-in), backface culling (skip unseen triangle faces—free in hardware), frustum culling (skip objects outside view cone). These are cheap and usually first in the pipeline.

Occlusion culling (hiding behind other objects) is harder but often highest-impact in dense scenes. Three approaches: hardware occlusion queries (render proxy geometry, read GPU results—one frame latency), software CPU-based culling (Intel's MSOC, zero latency but CPU-costly), Hi-Z depth pyramid (query fast, GPU-only, basis for most modern solutions). Two-pass Hi-Z pattern uses last frame's pyramid to cull, rebuilds fresh one from rendered survivors, retests previously-culled objects—avoids one-frame visibility lag except on hard camera cuts.

Screen-size culling, PVS (potentially visible sets—expensive to precompute but fast at runtime, rarely used now), and portal culling (for indoor scenes with well-defined doorways) round out the traditional arsenal.

GPU-driven rendering pushes culling logic onto GPU via indirect drawing: compute shader runs culling, atomically compacts survivors into a draw buffer, one indirect draw consumes it. Meshlet/cluster culling subdivides meshes into ~64-128 triangle clusters with bounding spheres and normal cones, enabling sub-mesh-level frustum, occlusion, and backface culling on GPU. Normal cones are clever: if all normals point roughly one way, reject entire cluster cheaply.

Nanite (UE5) builds a hierarchy of clusters where each level simplifies finer ones.
