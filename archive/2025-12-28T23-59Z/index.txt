12:Tc0f,The article walks through what a camera sensor truly captures: raw 14-bit analog-to-digital converter (ADC) values mapped to 0-255, which look like a nearly featureless gray image of a Christmas tree. The first lesson is histogram stretching—the ADC actually only uses a narrow range (2110-13600), so the author rescales to white and black points to reveal contrast.

Next comes the Bayer matrix revelation. Camera sensors can't see color; they measure total brightness. A color camera uses a grid of red, green, and blue filters, with green at 50% (RGGB pattern). This is justified not just for color balance but because the human eye is most sensitive to green—it carries the luminance (detail) channel while red/blue act as "color difference" layers. Demosaicing (averaging with neighbors) reconstructs full RGB at each pixel.

The dynamic range problem comes next. Monitors and eyes have limited dynamic range, and human perception of brightness is nonlinear. If you display linear sensor data directly, midtones appear far darker than they should. Gamma correction and tone curves fix this, but applying curves to each channel caused a green cast (worsened by the 2:1 green filter ratio). White balance (scaling each color channel by a constant) corrects this, but must be done in linear space before re-applying gamma.

The final result required: black point correction, gamma curves, white balance, and desaturation of highlights (a film-like effect). The "in-camera JPEG" is massively processed—not "fake," just a different rendering of the same data. The author emphasizes that replicating human perception within display constraints is hard; there's no shame in adjusting photos.

Comments add crucial context: The 50% green pattern isn't just about color but spatial resolution—demosaicing algorithms reconstruct green first to build a high-res luminance map, then interpolate red/blue.
