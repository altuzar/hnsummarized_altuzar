12:T11f7,The essay (dated August 2026 in a hypothetical future) observes that knowledge workers increasingly express hopelessness about their careers, trading office dreams for fantasies of escape—knitting, farming, disappearing. This isn't new economic anxiety; it's existential dread about the meaninglessness of the work itself.

Horwath traces this to "Workism," Derek Thompson's concept of treating work like religion—seeking community, identity, and purpose through careers that are often structurally pointless (moving money between rich people, debating irrelevant features, building projects that never launch). Workism worked as long as humans executed the work, creating at least an illusion of value through human effort and collaboration. AI shatters this illusion by automating even the abstracted busywork, pushing knowledge work so far from tangible reality that the spectacle becomes visible.

He applies Guy Debord's "Society of the Spectacle" theory: late capitalism mediated everything, replacing direct experience with representation. Workism exemplifies this—appearing important matters more than being important. AI intensifies the problem: you no longer even write the pitch; you prompt an AI to write it, then review it. The work becomes pure abstraction on top of abstraction.

The article identifies two worker types: outcome-first (embrace efficiency, see humans as obstacles) and experience-first (value messy collaboration, creativity, human connection). Harvard research shows experience-first workers do better creative work in collaborative, free environments. AI's promise of lean hierarchies and pure efficiency appeals to executives but devastates experience-first workers who relied on workplace community as compensation for meaningless work.

He warns that layoffs framed as AI-driven aren't actually about efficiency gains; executives are hoping to permanently reduce headcounts. If talent—particularly experience-first workers—loses faith and builds alternative lives, companies may struggle to rehire. Yet Debord warned that even rebellion gets absorbed back into the spectacle (think: ex-tech YouTubers documenting their farm life for algorithm engagement).

The essay ends with micro-resistance: choosing to call colleagues instead of querying AI, doing work "the old-fashioned way," deliberately preserving human connection, and maintaining awareness that Workism is an illusion.
