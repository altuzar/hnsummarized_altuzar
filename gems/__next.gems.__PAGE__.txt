29:Tc94,PenGPT generates handwriting and sketches using simple transformers on tokenized pen strokes. The 6M-parameter handwriting model learns the style of individual writers from just a few sample words—no fine-tuning required, just feeding multiple words from one writer during training so it learns "later words look like earlier ones." The 54M drawing model takes text prompts via SigLIP embeddings and generates sketches. Both use the same tokenizer: pen points snap to an 8-direction grid plus pen-up/down, then byte-pair encoding compresses common move sequences (reducing a word from 156 moves to 47 tokens).

The core contribution is methodological: data and execution matter far more than clever architecture. The handwriting data came from scanning 495k words across 1,255 writers from research collections and public archives (old letters, diaries). A vision-language model found legible pages, traced strokes with InkSight, and quality checks rejected ~50% of words. For drawings, the author cherry-picked 0.5% of Quick, Draw!'s 50 million sketches plus nine other datasets. Ablations are brutal: cutting data degrades everything; dropping augmentation breaks drawing entirely; training on one word at a time destroys style transfer. Meanwhile, the 60x vocabulary increase and 2x parameter doubling change nothing. BPE merges actually hurt the score slightly—they just buy 3.2x faster sequences for inference speed.

The model can pick up new handwriting from photos by tracing words with InkSight and fitting a new writer vector while freezing the rest of the model. Cross-attention analysis shows one head locks onto the current letter 81% of the time; most heads don't specialize in cursive vs. print. For drawings, captions prove essential—a picture-only model achieves better likelihood but generates scribbles because sketch embeddings sit in a narrow cone orthogonal to text vectors; labels alone get 78% label accuracy but only 43% phrase accuracy.
