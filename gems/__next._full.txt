2b:Te45,Carlini addresses a compelling counterfactual: could Deep Blue's 1997 victory over Kasparov have been achieved with smarter software on commodity 1996 desktop hardware instead of the $millions IBM spent on special-purpose processors? He tests this by actually obtaining a 200MHz Pentium Pro (costs ~$4-5k in 1996, ~$1k by 1997) and porting Stockfish to run on it, achieving 3300±87 Elo rating at classical time controls—compared to Kasparov's estimated 2800 Elo and Deep Blue's 2700. He instruments the machine with a custom OS on a floppy disk and Ethernet adapter so visitors can play it online.

The core technical claim: modern chess engines are ~1335 Elo stronger than 1997 engines. Of this, ~720 Elo comes from algorithmic improvements (better move selection, neural network evaluation, search pruning techniques) while only ~615 comes from hardware speedup. To demonstrate this, Carlini runs two experiments. First, he updates Crafty 11.13 (a 1996 competitive engine) by adding modern techniques: fail-soft search, late-move reduction, NNUE neural network evaluation with Stockfish-trained weights, parameter tuning via SPSA. This lifts it from 1999 Elo on the Pentium to 2846 Elo—approaching Deep Blue's level. Second, he ports modern Stockfish to the Pentium (rewriting for 32-bit arithmetic, 64MB RAM, no SIMD) and tunes the neural network and search parameters for the slow CPU, achieving 3109 Elo at 10 seconds per move.

Comments largely praise the execution but raise important caveats. rockmeamedee and vhcr note that the algorithmic gains heavily depend on NNUE (neural network evaluation) and tuning data, which required modern hardware and millions of games to develop in the first place—you can retroactively port NNUE to 1996 hardware, but 1996 programmers couldn't have invented it without access to modern compute. Sharlin corrects a minor error about Pentium MMX being "better" than Pro—the Pro was actually the more advanced P6 microarchitecture. dmurray points out the board display wrongly labels games as "Human vs Pentium" regardless of color, confusing the game flow. mb_thd complains the LLM disclosure ("I asked an LLM to write") appears halfway through, not upfront. The transformer applies one attention block plus ReLU MLP multiple times; increasing loop count trades speed for accuracy—most games use 2 loops for playable framerates.
