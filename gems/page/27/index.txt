1. Download splats from SuperSplat (Creative Commons tagged).
2. Convert to streamed SOG format with splat-transform CLI to avoid stalling mobile and slow connections via on-demand chunk loading.
3. Generate collision mesh using splat-transform's voxelization: voxelize the splat, flood-fill the navigable interior, output a watertight GLB imported as a static rigidbody.
4. Bake a lightness grid by rendering 6-face cube probes at 1m intervals, sampling the splat alone, computing luminance per probe, storing in JSON. At runtime, dynamic mesh instances sample this grid bilinearly to inherit the splat's lighting without expensive probes or relighting.
5. Iterate with PlayCanvas VS Code extension (works in Cursor) for fast save-reload cycles.
6. Version control via PlayCanvas + GitHub.
7. Generate navmesh from the collision GLB using recast-navigation loaded from esm.sh (library coming soon).
8. Implement NPC AI with four-function behavior trees (sequence/selector/condition/action), parameterized by personality traits (aggression, heal priority, loot priority, etc.). Eight NPCs with different trait profiles feel distinct despite using identical tree structure.

33:T9f4,The article explains GPS as fundamentally a time-to-distance converter: satellites broadcast signals at light speed, your phone measures transit delay, and distance equals speed times time. One satellite gives you a sphere of possible positions (a ring on Earth's surface). Two satellites produce two intersection points. Three satellites narrow it to one location via trilateration.

The key problem: phone clocks drift by microseconds, and nanosecond errors cause meter-scale position errors. The solution uses a fourth satellite. With four unknowns (three position coordinates plus clock error), the system solves for all simultaneously—the only clock correction where all four spheres intersect at a single point snaps everything into place.

Crucially, relativistic effects dominate. Satellites orbit at ~3.8km/s and sit far from Earth's gravity well. Special relativity predicts their clocks lose ~38 nanoseconds daily due to orbital speed. General relativity predicts they gain ~45 nanoseconds daily from weaker gravity. Net gain: ~7 nanoseconds daily, which translates to ~2.1 meters of position drift per 24 hours without correction. The engineering solution is elegant: satellite atomic clocks are deliberately built to tick slightly slow on the ground so orbital mechanics brings them to the correct rate in space. Without these relativistic corrections, GPS becomes unusable within hours—modern accuracy within meters is a continuous proof Einstein was right.

Modern receivers use 8-12 satellites, listen to multiple systems (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou), and optimize for geometric dilution of precision (GDOP)—satellites clumped in one sky region produce shallow intersection angles and uncertainty.
