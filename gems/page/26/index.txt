12:T10ff,The author documented building a DIY permanent hair removal device using galvanic electrolysis, the FDA-approved permanent method. Unlike laser hair removal (only reduction), galvanic electrolysis inserts a needle into the follicle and applies DC current to generate lye (sodium hydroxide) that kills the follicle. The author chose galvanic over RF thermolysis because it's simpler and lower-risk for burns.

The project evolved through three hardware revisions. The first proof-of-concept was deliberately crude: needle held in alligator clips, connected through a potentiometer and car battery to a pop can electrode, with manual timing by counting seconds. Despite its janky appearance, it successfully removed hair. The author then designed a custom electrolysis pen by discovering that female DE-9 connector pins fit needles snugly, mounted in a 3D-printed housing.

The main machine design centered on automation and safety. The circuit uses a Dickson charge pump (3 stages, no separate power supply) to boost 3.7V to 15V, since body tissue has high impedance. A 5-bit resistor DAC provides microcontroller-set reference voltage; an op-amp comparator and BJT transistor create a constant-current source. The RP2040 measures actual current via ADC to verify lye generation (1 mA × 1 s = 10 lye units), ramps current up/down to reduce pain, and auto-stops when target lye is reached. A foot pedal triggers the pulse. Intentional design constraint: the low-power charge pump caps maximum current at a few milliamps for inherent safety.

Revision 2 had major issues discovered too late: charge pump GPIO surge damaged RP2040 pins (worked around with bodge wires and external supply); BJT base leakage corrupted current measurements; 5-bit DAC lacked fidelity; RP2040 ADC was inaccurate; silkscreen errors including component name swaps. Revision 3 replaced the BJT with a MOSFET, added GPIO protection resistors, used dedicated DAC and ADC chips (vastly improved), increased charge pump to 4 stages for 15V, reduced board size to 53×53mm with cleaner layout, and added decorative transgender pride silkscreen.

The author tested extensively on themselves, discovering that proper needle insertion into the follicle felt painless (needle enters existing hole), though "bottoming out" in the follicle was uncomfortable but not painful. The electricity itself hurt slightly more than laser but less than feared. Resistance drops as lye forms, and lye acts as lubricant. Missed insertions cause mosquito-bite-like swelling; successful ones show slight lye leakage. The author improved insertion accuracy over time. Firmware was written in Rust using Embassy async framework; the author appreciated Embassy's cooperative multitasking model more than expected and included cute audio feedback (a scale sweep during self-test).

37:T9f4,The article explains GPS as fundamentally a time-to-distance converter: satellites broadcast signals at light speed, your phone measures transit delay, and distance equals speed times time. One satellite gives you a sphere of possible positions (a ring on Earth's surface). Two satellites produce two intersection points. Three satellites narrow it to one location via trilateration.

The key problem: phone clocks drift by microseconds, and nanosecond errors cause meter-scale position errors. The solution uses a fourth satellite. With four unknowns (three position coordinates plus clock error), the system solves for all simultaneously—the only clock correction where all four spheres intersect at a single point snaps everything into place.

Crucially, relativistic effects dominate. Satellites orbit at ~3.8km/s and sit far from Earth's gravity well. Special relativity predicts their clocks lose ~38 nanoseconds daily due to orbital speed. General relativity predicts they gain ~45 nanoseconds daily from weaker gravity. Net gain: ~7 nanoseconds daily, which translates to ~2.1 meters of position drift per 24 hours without correction. The engineering solution is elegant: satellite atomic clocks are deliberately built to tick slightly slow on the ground so orbital mechanics brings them to the correct rate in space. Without these relativistic corrections, GPS becomes unusable within hours—modern accuracy within meters is a continuous proof Einstein was right.

Modern receivers use 8-12 satellites, listen to multiple systems (GPS, GLONASS, Galileo, BeiDou), and optimize for geometric dilution of precision (GDOP)—satellites clumped in one sky region produce shallow intersection angles and uncertainty.
