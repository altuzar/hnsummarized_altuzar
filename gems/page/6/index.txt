1f:T86d,Charles Petzold explores the tantalizing cover of "Calculating with Tones," which suggests guitar frets are spaced identically to logarithmic slide rule scales, implying multiplication is possible. He constructs interactive demos showing how frets might multiply: label the nut as 1 and the 12th fret as 2, then numbers between them as fractions. Sliding two "guitar halves" together, some products work: 7/6 × 3/2 = 7/4. But extending to two octaves breaks completely—3/2 × 2 should equal 3, yet the result falls short.

Petzold then explains the mathematics. Slide rules work because numbers are positioned by their logarithm: distance = log(n). Guitar frets, by contrast, must reduce string length by 2^(-1/12) per semitone to achieve equal-temperament tuning (12 equal steps per octave). This gives the formula: tick distance = 2 - 2^(n-2). When graphed against the logarithmic scale, these functions coincide only between 1 and 2; they diverge everywhere else. Frets exploit a mathematical accident in the first octave that evaporates beyond it.

The conclusion: despite surface resemblance, guitar frets do not perform multiplication. Music's perceptual logarithmicity (humans hear octaves as equal steps) drives fret spacing, not slide rule design. Petzold's interactive rulers and comparison graphs illuminate why the illusion works locally but fails globally.
