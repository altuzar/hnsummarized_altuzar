19:T90f,Ken Shirriff's blog post details the reverse-engineered circuitry of the 69-bit adder central to the 8087, a chip that made math 100x faster when released in 1980. The article explains how the adder achieves performance by breaking addition into 4-bit blocks rather than waiting for carry ripple across all 69 bits.

The core technique is the Manchester carry chain, developed at Manchester University in 1959. Before addition, the circuit pre-computes Generate (both inputs 1), Propagate (one input 1), and Delete (both inputs 0) signals for each bit position in parallel, then lets the carry signal propagate through switched connections at wire speed rather than through logic gates. Each 4-bit block uses a carry-skip optimization: if all bits in a block propagate, the carry-out equals carry-in without traversing the full chain. A 4-input multiplexer selects between propagate, generate, and skip paths.

The implementation exploits NMOS transistor physics: transistors pull signals low faster than high, so the carry lines are precharged to 5V and pulled low as needed. Carries are inverted so 5V means no carry. The adder takes 69-bit inputs (64-bit fraction plus guard/round/sticky bits plus doubled B register plus two's complement negation) and produces 70 bits. Despite these optimizations, the 8087 still requires two clock cycles for addition.

Shirriff provides reverse-engineered schematics showing the actual multiplexer and NMOS transistor implementations, plus microscopy of the die with and without the metal layer dissolved, revealing polysilicon routing and doped silicon regions where transistors form.

3e:Tb9c,Lotte Reiniger (1899–1981) began her film career handling rats on a 1920s adaptation of The Pied Piper. That production's switch to wooden stop-motion models inspired her own innovation: using articulated cardboard and lead silhouette figures, lit from below and photographed frame-by-frame from above. By 1919 she'd completed her first short; a Berlin banker financed a feature in 1923, extraordinary for the era.

Released in May 1926, "The Adventures of Prince Achmed" combined Middle Eastern fairy tales into a mythological narrative. Reiniger directed, designed, cut all puppets, and devised the scenario with a tiny crew including her husband who operated the camera. The film took three years to complete and required roughly 1,000 frames per minute. Its standout technical achievement was an early multiplane camera: multiple glass layers held silhouettes and backgrounds, moved at different speeds to create depth—a technique Reiniger explained in her 1970 book "Shadow Puppets, Shadow Theatres and Shadow Films." Walt Disney received a US patent for the multiplane camera in 1940 and was inducted into the National Inventors Hall of Fame in 2000 for "inventing" it, despite Reiniger pioneering the approach.

The film reached audiences in France, UK, and US, earning critical praise (Jean Renoir compared her to Mozart). Disney's marketing prowess made that claim stick in popular memory, though Prince Achmed predates it by 11 years.
